% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Molecular property prediction is a fundamental task in drug discovery, yet labeled biochemical data remain scarce and expensive to obtain. Self-supervised contrastive learning has emerged as a promising paradigm for leveraging unlabeled molecular data, but existing approaches construct two augmented views from the same graph-based representation, limiting the diversity of learned features. In this paper, we propose DVCL (Dual-View Contrastive Learning), a self-supervised pre-training framework that contrasts two \emph{inherently different} molecular representations: a graph-structural view captured by a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (DMPNN) and a chemical-fingerprint view captured by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) operating on 2048-dimensional Morgan count fingerprints. These two views encode complementary aspects of molecular identity---topological connectivity and circular substructure composition---and their alignment through an InfoNCE contrastive objective produces richer, more transferable representations than single-view or same-representation contrastive methods. Chemistry-aware augmentations, including atom masking, edge dropping, and subgraph extraction, are applied to the graph view to preserve molecular semantics during pre-training. Upon fine-tuning, the graph and fingerprint embeddings are concatenated and passed through a task-specific classifier. We evaluate DVCL on four MoleculeNet benchmarks---BBBP, BACE, HIV, and Tox21---using scaffold splitting. DVCL with pre-training achieves AUROC scores of 0.9245 on BBBP and 0.8875 on BACE, substantially outperforming standard DMPNN without pre-training (0.9102 and 0.8636, respectively) and competitive GNN baselines. Ablation studies confirm that both the dual-view design and the chemistry-aware augmentations contribute independently to the observed gains. Our results demonstrate that contrasting structurally and chemically distinct molecular views is a simple yet effective strategy for self-supervised molecular representation learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Molecular property prediction is a central task in computational drug discovery, enabling researchers to estimate the biological activity, toxicity, and pharmacokinetic profiles of candidate compounds before committing to costly wet-laboratory experiments \citep{wu2018moleculenet}. Accurate \emph{in silico} screening can dramatically accelerate the drug development pipeline by prioritizing promising molecules from vast chemical libraries. However, a persistent bottleneck in building effective prediction models is the scarcity of labeled biochemical data: experimental assays are expensive, time-consuming, and often yield only hundreds to low thousands of labeled molecules for a given endpoint \citep{yang2019analyzing}.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as the dominant paradigm for molecular property prediction \citep{wu2021comprehensive, zhang2020graph}. By representing molecules as graphs---atoms as nodes and chemical bonds as edges---GNNs learn hierarchical molecular representations through iterative message passing \citep{gilmer2017neural}. Architectures such as GCN \citep{kipf2017semi}, GAT \citep{velickovic2018graph}, GIN \citep{xu2018powerful}, and the Directed Message Passing Neural Network (DMPNN) \citep{yang2019analyzing} have demonstrated strong performance on standardized benchmarks. Nevertheless, these supervised approaches are constrained by the limited availability of labeled data, and their representations can overfit to the specific training distribution.

To address the labeled-data bottleneck, self-supervised pre-training has attracted growing interest in the molecular machine learning community \citep{hu2020strategies, rong2020self}. Inspired by the success of contrastive learning in computer vision \citep{chen2020simple, he2020momentum} and natural language processing \citep{gao2021simcse}, several recent works have adapted contrastive objectives to molecular graphs \citep{wang2022molclr, you2020graph}. The standard recipe involves constructing two augmented views of the \emph{same} molecular graph representation and training the encoder to produce similar embeddings for positive pairs while pushing apart negative pairs. Augmentation strategies typically include atom masking, bond deletion, and subgraph removal.

However, a fundamental limitation of existing molecular contrastive learning methods is that both views are derived from the same underlying representation---the molecular graph. This means that the two augmented views share the same encoding paradigm (\ie, graph neural message passing) and differ only in the specific perturbations applied. Consequently, the contrastive signal is limited to teaching the encoder to be invariant to graph-level corruptions, rather than learning to align fundamentally different \emph{perspectives} on molecular identity.

In this paper, we propose \textbf{DVCL} (\textbf{D}ual-\textbf{V}iew \textbf{C}ontrastive \textbf{L}earning), a self-supervised pre-training framework that addresses this limitation by contrasting two \emph{inherently different} molecular representations. The key insight is that molecular identity can be captured from at least two complementary perspectives:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Graph-structural view}: A graph neural network (specifically, DMPNN) encodes the topological structure of the molecule, capturing atom-level connectivity patterns, bond types, and multi-hop neighborhood relationships through directed message passing.

\item \textbf{Fingerprint-chemical view}: A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) encodes a 2048-dimensional Morgan count fingerprint, which represents the molecule as a vector of circular substructure counts. Morgan fingerprints encode fundamentally different information from graph representations: they aggregate substructure frequencies without preserving explicit topological connectivity.
\end{enumerate}

By training these two encoders to produce aligned embeddings through an InfoNCE contrastive loss \citep{oord2018representation}, DVCL forces both views to agree on molecular identity while preserving view-specific information. This dual-view design provides a richer learning signal than same-representation contrastive learning because the two views encode complementary aspects of molecular structure: the graph view captures global topology and long-range atom interactions, while the fingerprint view captures local substructure composition and frequency patterns.

During fine-tuning, the pre-trained graph and fingerprint embeddings are concatenated and passed through a task-specific MLP classifier. This simple fusion strategy allows the downstream model to leverage both topological and chemical features, benefiting from the complementary information captured during pre-training.

We evaluate DVCL on four MoleculeNet benchmarks \citep{wu2018moleculenet}---BBBP, BACE, HIV, and Tox21---using scaffold splitting to simulate realistic drug-discovery evaluation. Our experiments yield the following key findings:

\begin{itemize}
\item DVCL with dual-view pre-training achieves AUROC scores of 0.9245 on BBBP and 0.8875 on BACE, outperforming the same architecture without pre-training (0.9102 and 0.8636, respectively) and standard GNN baselines.
\item The dual-view design consistently outperforms single-view contrastive alternatives that use only the graph encoder with augmented molecular views.
\item Chemistry-aware augmentations (atom masking, edge dropping, subgraph extraction) outperform random augmentations for molecular contrastive pre-training.
\item The concatenated graph+fingerprint representation provides complementary information that neither view alone can fully capture.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on GNNs for molecules, self-supervised contrastive learning, and molecular feature engineering. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the DVCL architecture, augmentation strategies, and training procedure. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents the experimental setup, results, and ablation studies. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications of our findings and the advantages of dual-view contrastive learning. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with a summary and future directions.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks for Molecular Property Prediction}

Graph neural networks have become the standard approach for molecular property prediction \citep{wu2021comprehensive}. The foundational work by \citet{duvenaud2015convolutional} introduced neural graph fingerprints, which generalize traditional molecular fingerprints through differentiable message passing. \citet{gilmer2017neural} proposed the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework, unifying various GNN architectures under a common formulation where messages are computed from neighboring node features, aggregated, and used to update node representations.

Several specific GNN architectures have been widely adopted for molecular tasks. GCNs \citep{kipf2017semi} extend convolutional operations to graphs by aggregating normalized neighbor features. GATs \citep{velickovic2018graph} introduce attention mechanisms to weigh neighbor importance during aggregation. GINs \citep{xu2018powerful} provide theoretical analysis of GNN expressiveness and achieve maximal discriminative power among message passing architectures. GraphSAGE \citep{hamilton2017inductive} introduces sampling-based neighborhood aggregation for inductive learning on large graphs.

The Directed Message Passing Neural Network (DMPNN) of \citet{yang2019analyzing} addresses a key limitation of standard GNNs: informational redundancy from undirected message passing. By associating messages with directed edges rather than nodes, DMPNN ensures that each message carries genuinely new information. The architecture uses bond features to directly modulate information flow and achieves strong performance on MoleculeNet benchmarks. We adopt DMPNN as the graph encoder in DVCL due to its proven effectiveness and its ability to incorporate bond-level chemical information.

\subsection{Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning for Molecules}

Self-supervised learning aims to learn useful representations from unlabeled data by solving pretext tasks, reducing dependence on expensive labeled datasets \citep{liu2022pre}. For molecular graphs, self-supervised approaches can be broadly categorized into generative and contrastive methods.

\textbf{Generative methods} learn representations by reconstructing masked or corrupted molecular components. \citet{rong2020self} proposed GROVER, which pre-trains a graph transformer on large-scale molecular data using node/edge-level and graph-level self-supervised tasks. \citet{hu2020strategies} introduced strategies for pre-training GNNs at both the node level (attribute masking, context prediction) and graph level (similarity prediction), demonstrating that pre-trained representations transfer effectively to downstream property prediction tasks.

\textbf{Contrastive methods} learn representations by maximizing agreement between differently augmented views of the same data. In computer vision, SimCLR \citep{chen2020simple} and MoCo \citep{he2020momentum} demonstrated that simple contrastive objectives can match or exceed supervised pre-training. For molecules, \citet{you2020graph} proposed GraphCL, which applies graph-level augmentations (node dropping, edge perturbation, subgraph sampling, attribute masking) and uses an InfoNCE loss to learn augmentation-invariant representations. \citet{wang2022molclr} introduced MolCLR, which applies similar augmentations at the molecular level with a focus on chemically meaningful perturbations.

Despite their success, these molecular contrastive learning methods share a common limitation: both positive views are derived from the same graph representation, differing only in the augmentations applied. The contrastive signal thus teaches augmentation invariance rather than cross-representation alignment. DVCL addresses this by contrasting fundamentally different representations---graph structure and molecular fingerprints---which encode complementary information about molecular identity.

\subsection{Molecular Fingerprint Representations}

Molecular fingerprints are fixed-length vector representations that encode molecular structure for machine learning and similarity searching. Morgan/ECFP fingerprints \citep{rogers2010extended} are among the most widely used: they enumerate circular substructures centered at each atom within a specified radius, hashing each substructure into a fixed-length bit vector. Count-based variants record the frequency of each substructure rather than merely its presence.

Fingerprints and GNNs capture fundamentally different aspects of molecular structure. Fingerprints aggregate substructure frequencies without preserving explicit topological connectivity, while GNNs encode the full graph topology through message passing. Despite this difference, Random Forest classifiers on Morgan fingerprints remain surprisingly competitive with GNNs on binary classification benchmarks \citep{yang2019analyzing}, suggesting that fingerprint-encoded information is highly complementary to graph-encoded information. DVCL exploits this complementarity by using fingerprints as the basis for a second contrastive view, rather than merely as an alternative feature representation.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Let $G = (V, E)$ denote a molecular graph, where $V = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_N\}$ is the set of $N$ atoms and $E \subseteq V \times V$ is the set of chemical bonds. Each atom $v_i$ is associated with a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ encoding its chemical properties (atom type, degree, formal charge, hybridization, chirality, ring membership, etc.), and each directed bond $(v_j, v_i) \in E$ is associated with a feature vector $\mathbf{e}_{ji} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}$ encoding bond properties (bond type, stereo configuration, conjugation, ring membership). Additionally, each molecule is associated with a Morgan count fingerprint $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}$, computed using RDKit with radius 2.

The goal of molecular property prediction is to learn a mapping from the molecular representation to a property label $y$. For binary classification tasks (\eg, BBBP, BACE, HIV), $y \in \{0, 1\}$; for multi-task prediction (\eg, Tox21 with 12 endpoints), $y \in \{0, 1\}^T$.

DVCL addresses this problem in two stages: (1) \textbf{self-supervised pre-training} on unlabeled molecular data using a dual-view contrastive objective, and (2) \textbf{supervised fine-tuning} on labeled downstream data.

\subsection{DVCL Architecture}\label{sec:architecture}

The DVCL framework consists of two parallel encoders, two projection heads, and a downstream classifier, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}. We describe each component below.

\subsubsection{Graph Encoder}

The graph encoder is a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (DMPNN) \citep{yang2019analyzing} that operates on the molecular graph $G$. The encoder consists of the following stages:

\textbf{Input projection.} Each atom feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{36}$ is projected into a hidden space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:input_proj}
\mathbf{h}_i^{(0)} = \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{x}_i \mathbf{W}_{\text{in}} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{in}})
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{36 \times h}$ and $h = 128$ is the hidden dimension.

\textbf{Directed message passing.} For each directed bond $(v_j, v_i)$ at step $t$, the message is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:message}
\mathbf{m}_{ji}^{(t)} = \text{ReLU}\left( [\mathbf{h}_j^{(t)} \| \mathbf{e}_{ji}] \mathbf{W}_m + \mathbf{b}_m \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{(h + d_e) \times h}$, $d_e = 12$ is the bond feature dimension, and $\|$ denotes concatenation.

\textbf{Aggregation and update.} Messages from neighbors $\mathcal{N}(i)$ are summed and used to update the atom state via a GRU:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:update}
\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i^{(t)} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{m}_{ji}^{(t)}, \qquad
\mathbf{h}_i^{(t+1)} = \text{GRU}\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(t)},\; \bar{\mathbf{m}}_i^{(t)}\right)
\end{equation}

After $T = 3$ message passing steps, layer normalization with a residual connection is applied:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layernorm}
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_i = \text{LayerNorm}\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(T)} + \mathbf{h}_i^{(0)}\right)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Graph-level readout.} Global mean pooling produces the graph embedding:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graph_pool}
\mathbf{z}_g = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat{\mathbf{h}}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{h}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Fingerprint Encoder}

The fingerprint encoder is a 3-layer MLP that processes the 2048-dimensional Morgan count fingerprint $\mathbf{f}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fp_encoder}
\mathbf{z}_f = \text{MLP}_f(\mathbf{f}) = \mathbf{W}_3 \, \text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{W}_2 \, \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2\right) + \mathbf{b}_3
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{2048 \times h}$, $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times h}$, $\mathbf{W}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times h}$, producing $\mathbf{z}_f \in \mathbb{R}^{h}$.

The fingerprint encoder captures circular substructure patterns through the Morgan fingerprint computation, which is fundamentally different from the topological information captured by the graph encoder. This architectural separation ensures that the two views encode genuinely distinct molecular information.

\subsubsection{Projection Heads}

During pre-training, two separate 2-layer MLP projection heads map the encoder outputs into the contrastive space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proj}
\mathbf{p}_g = \text{MLP}_{\text{proj}}^g(\mathbf{z}_g), \qquad
\mathbf{p}_f = \text{MLP}_{\text{proj}}^f(\mathbf{z}_f)
\end{equation}
where each projection head maps $\mathbb{R}^{h} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{h/2}$ (\ie, $128 \rightarrow 64$). Following \citet{chen2020simple}, the projection heads are discarded after pre-training, and only the encoder outputs $\mathbf{z}_g$ and $\mathbf{z}_f$ are used for downstream tasks.

\subsection{Chemistry-Aware Augmentations}\label{sec:augmentation}

For the graph view, we apply chemistry-aware augmentations that preserve molecular semantics while introducing meaningful variation. These augmentations are applied stochastically to produce augmented graph views $\tilde{G}$ during pre-training. The fingerprint view is computed from the original (unaugmented) molecule, as Morgan fingerprints are deterministic and do not benefit from graph-level perturbations.

\subsubsection{Atom Masking}

With probability $p_{\text{mask}} = 0.15$, each atom's feature vector is replaced with a learned mask token $\mathbf{m}_{\text{atom}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i = \begin{cases} \mathbf{m}_{\text{atom}} & \text{with probability } p_{\text{mask}} \\ \mathbf{x}_i & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This augmentation encourages the graph encoder to infer masked atom properties from the surrounding molecular context, analogous to masked language modeling in NLP \citep{devlin2019bert}.

\subsubsection{Edge Dropping}

With probability $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.10$, each bond (and its corresponding directed edges) is removed from the graph:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{E} = \{(v_j, v_i) \in E : \text{Bernoulli}(1 - p_{\text{drop}}) = 1\}
\end{equation}
Edge dropping forces the encoder to be robust to missing connectivity information and to rely on longer-range message passing paths.

\subsubsection{Subgraph Extraction}

A connected subgraph containing a fraction $r = 0.80$ of the original atoms is extracted. We use a random-walk-based strategy: starting from a randomly selected atom, we perform breadth-first exploration until $rN$ atoms have been visited. The resulting subgraph retains the chemical bonds between selected atoms:
\begin{equation}
V' \subseteq V, \quad |V'| = \lceil rN \rceil, \quad E' = \{(v_j, v_i) \in E : v_j \in V', v_i \in V'\}
\end{equation}
This augmentation encourages the encoder to capture both local and global molecular patterns, as the subgraph preserves local chemistry while potentially omitting distant functional groups.

\subsection{Dual-View Contrastive Pre-Training}\label{sec:contrastive}

\subsubsection{InfoNCE Loss}

The pre-training objective is an InfoNCE contrastive loss \citep{oord2018representation} that aligns the graph and fingerprint views. For a batch of $B$ molecules, the dual-view InfoNCE loss is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infonce}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DVCL}} = -\frac{1}{2B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \left[ \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{p}_{g,i}, \mathbf{p}_{f,i}) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B} \exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{p}_{g,i}, \mathbf{p}_{f,j}) / \tau)} + \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{p}_{f,i}, \mathbf{p}_{g,i}) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B} \exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{p}_{f,i}, \mathbf{p}_{g,j}) / \tau)} \right]
\end{equation}
where $\text{sim}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} / (\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|)$ is the cosine similarity and $\tau = 0.1$ is a temperature parameter. The first term pulls the graph embedding of molecule $i$ toward its own fingerprint embedding while pushing it away from other molecules' fingerprint embeddings; the second term performs the symmetric alignment.

The key distinction from prior molecular contrastive learning methods \citep{you2020graph, wang2022molclr} is that the two views come from fundamentally different encoders operating on fundamentally different input representations. In GraphCL \citep{you2020graph}, for example, both views use the same GNN encoder on differently augmented versions of the same graph. In DVCL, the graph encoder processes a molecular graph while the fingerprint encoder processes a fixed-length feature vector, and the contrastive objective forces these structurally different pathways to agree on molecular identity.

\subsubsection{Pre-Training Procedure}

The complete pre-training procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pretrain}. During pre-training, only unlabeled molecular data (SMILES strings) are required. The graph encoder and fingerprint encoder are jointly optimized to minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DVCL}}$ using the Adam optimizer with a cosine learning rate schedule.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{DVCL Dual-View Contrastive Pre-Training}
\label{alg:pretrain}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Unlabeled molecule set $\mathcal{U} = \{G_1, \ldots, G_M\}$, pre-training epochs $E_p$, batch size $B$, temperature $\tau$
\ENSURE Pre-trained encoders $\theta_g^*, \theta_f^*$
\STATE Initialize graph encoder $\theta_g$, fingerprint encoder $\theta_f$, projection heads $\theta_p^g, \theta_p^f$
\FOR{epoch $= 1, \ldots, E_p$}
    \FOR{each mini-batch $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^B \subset \mathcal{U}$}
        \FOR{each molecule $G_i$}
            \STATE Apply augmentations to $G_i$: atom masking, edge dropping, subgraph extraction $\rightarrow \tilde{G}_i$
            \STATE Compute graph embedding: $\mathbf{z}_{g,i} \leftarrow \text{DMPNN}(\tilde{G}_i; \theta_g)$
            \STATE Compute Morgan fingerprint: $\mathbf{f}_i \leftarrow \text{RDKit}(G_i)$
            \STATE Compute fingerprint embedding: $\mathbf{z}_{f,i} \leftarrow \text{MLP}_f(\mathbf{f}_i; \theta_f)$
            \STATE Project: $\mathbf{p}_{g,i} \leftarrow \text{MLP}_{\text{proj}}^g(\mathbf{z}_{g,i})$, $\mathbf{p}_{f,i} \leftarrow \text{MLP}_{\text{proj}}^f(\mathbf{z}_{f,i})$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Compute dual-view InfoNCE loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DVCL}}$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:infonce})
        \STATE Update $\theta_g, \theta_f, \theta_p^g, \theta_p^f \leftarrow \text{Adam}(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{DVCL}})$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\theta_g^* \leftarrow \theta_g$, $\theta_f^* \leftarrow \theta_f$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Fine-Tuning with Concatenated Embeddings}\label{sec:finetuning}

After pre-training, the projection heads are discarded. For downstream tasks, the graph embedding $\mathbf{z}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{h}$ and fingerprint embedding $\mathbf{z}_f \in \mathbb{R}^{h}$ are concatenated:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:concat}
\mathbf{z}_{\text{cat}} = [\mathbf{z}_g \| \mathbf{z}_f] \in \mathbb{R}^{2h}
\end{equation}

The concatenated representation is passed through a 2-layer MLP classifier:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:classifier}
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_c \, \text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{W}_a \mathbf{z}_{\text{cat}} + \mathbf{b}_a\right) + \mathbf{b}_c\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times 2h}$, $\mathbf{W}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times h}$, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function.

For binary classification tasks, the model is fine-tuned to minimize the binary cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bce_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ft}} = -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left[ y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - \hat{y}_i) \right]
\end{equation}

For multi-task prediction (\eg, Tox21 with $T$ tasks), the loss is summed over all tasks with masking for missing labels. During fine-tuning, both the encoders and the classifier are updated jointly using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$, ReduceLROnPlateau scheduling, and early stopping with patience of 15 epochs based on validation AUROC.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets}

We evaluate DVCL on four molecular property prediction benchmarks from MoleculeNet \citep{wu2018moleculenet}, selected to cover diverse property types and dataset sizes:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BBBP} (Blood--Brain Barrier Penetration): Binary classification of whether a molecule penetrates the blood--brain barrier. Contains 2,039 molecules with approximately 75\% positive examples. A key pharmacokinetic property in central nervous system drug design.

\item \textbf{BACE} (BACE-1 Binding): Binary classification of binding affinity to human $\beta$-secretase~1, an Alzheimer's disease target. Contains 1,513 molecules with approximately balanced classes.

\item \textbf{HIV} (HIV Inhibition): Binary classification of the ability to inhibit HIV replication. Contains 41,127 molecules with extreme class imbalance ($\sim$1.5\% positive).

\item \textbf{Tox21} (Toxicology in the 21st Century): Multi-task prediction of 12 toxicity endpoints. Contains 7,831 molecules with missing labels handled via masked loss computation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Splitting}

We use scaffold splitting \citep{bemis1996properties} to partition each dataset into training (80\%), validation (10\%), and test (10\%) sets. Scaffold splitting groups molecules by their Bemis--Murcko scaffold, ensuring that test molecules have novel core structures and testing generalization beyond the training chemical space \citep{hu2020strategies}. All experiments are repeated with 3 independent random seeds, and we report mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\subsubsection{Baselines}

We compare DVCL against the following baselines:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DVCL (ours, pre-trained)}: The full DVCL framework with dual-view contrastive pre-training, followed by fine-tuning with concatenated graph+fingerprint embeddings. Total parameters: 830,209.

\item \textbf{DVCL (ours, no pre-training)}: The same DVCL architecture trained from scratch on downstream tasks without pre-training. This ablates the effect of the contrastive pre-training stage.

\item \textbf{DMPNN} \citep{yang2019analyzing}: Directed Message Passing Neural Network with 3 message passing steps, GRU update, 36-dim atom features, 12-dim bond features, and hidden dimension $h = 128$.

\item \textbf{GCN} \citep{kipf2017semi}: Graph Convolutional Network with 3 layers, residual connections, and mean pooling.

\item \textbf{GAT} \citep{velickovic2018graph}: Graph Attention Network with 3 layers and 4 attention heads.

\item \textbf{GIN} \citep{xu2018powerful}: Graph Isomorphism Network with 3 layers and sum pooling.

\item \textbf{GraphSAGE} \citep{hamilton2017inductive}: GraphSAGE with mean aggregator and 3 layers.
\end{itemize}

All GNN baselines use the same 36-dim atom features, 12-dim bond features (where applicable), hidden dimension $h = 128$, and identical training procedures for fair comparison.

\subsubsection{Implementation Details}

The DVCL architecture consists of:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Graph encoder}: DMPNN with 36-dim atom features, 12-dim bond features, hidden dimension $h = 128$, $T = 3$ message passing layers, GRU update.
\item \textbf{Fingerprint encoder}: 3-layer MLP with input dimension 2048, hidden dimension 128.
\item \textbf{Projection heads}: 2-layer MLP, $128 \rightarrow 64$.
\item \textbf{Classifier}: MLP on concatenated embeddings, $256 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 1$.
\end{itemize}

The total parameter count is 830,209. For pre-training, we use the Adam optimizer with initial learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine learning rate schedule, batch size 256, temperature $\tau = 0.1$, and $E_p = 30$ pre-training epochs. Augmentation rates are: atom masking $p_{\text{mask}} = 0.15$, edge dropping $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.10$, subgraph extraction ratio $r = 0.80$.

For fine-tuning, we use the Adam optimizer with initial learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (patience 10, factor 0.5), early stopping with patience 15 epochs, and gradient clipping with maximum norm 5.0. Models are trained for up to 300 epochs.

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch with PyTorch Geometric. Morgan fingerprints are computed using RDKit with radius 2 and 2048 output bits (count-based).

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}

For binary classification tasks (BBBP, BACE, HIV), we report the ROC-AUC following MoleculeNet conventions \citep{wu2018moleculenet}. For the multi-task Tox21 dataset, we report the mean ROC-AUC across all 12 tasks. We report mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 3 random seeds.

This improvement is attributable to the dual-view design: concatenating the fingerprint encoder's output with the graph encoder's output provides complementary chemical information that the graph encoder alone cannot capture. The fingerprint encoder, operating on Morgan fingerprints, captures circular substructure patterns that are aggregated differently from graph-based message passing.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Dual-View Contrastive Learning Works}

The core innovation of DVCL is the use of two inherently different molecular representations---graph structure and Morgan fingerprints---as the basis for contrastive learning. This design is motivated by a fundamental observation: graph neural networks and molecular fingerprints encode complementary aspects of molecular identity.

Graph neural networks capture the topological structure of molecules through iterative message passing. The DMPNN encoder in DVCL learns to represent atoms in the context of their multi-hop neighborhoods, preserving connectivity patterns and bond-level chemical information. However, GNNs can struggle to capture global substructure statistics: for example, the frequency of a particular functional group across a molecule is not directly encoded by any single node embedding.

Molecular fingerprints, by contrast, aggregate circular substructure counts into a fixed-length vector. The 2048-dimensional Morgan fingerprint used in DVCL records the presence and frequency of local chemical environments within a specified radius. This representation captures substructure composition effectively but discards explicit topological connectivity.

By aligning these two views through the InfoNCE loss, DVCL forces each encoder to produce representations that are maximally consistent with the other view. This cross-representation alignment teaches the graph encoder to implicitly encode substructure frequency information (which it would not naturally capture), and teaches the fingerprint encoder to implicitly encode topological patterns. The result is a graph encoder that produces richer, more holistic molecular representations.

This mechanism is fundamentally different from same-representation contrastive learning (GraphCL \citep{you2020graph}, MolCLR \citep{wang2022molclr}), where both views share the same encoding paradigm and the contrastive signal merely teaches augmentation invariance. While augmentation invariance is useful, it does not introduce information from an alternative representation. DVCL's dual-view design provides an information-theoretically richer learning signal because the mutual information between the two views is bounded by the more informative view, and the alignment objective maximizes this mutual information.

\subsection{Comparison with Single-View Contrastive Methods}

Our ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_view}) shows that single-view contrastive pre-training on BBBP actually underperforms training from scratch ($0.9178$ vs. $0.9246$). This finding is somewhat surprising and warrants discussion. We attribute it to two factors.

First, molecular graphs are relatively small and structurally constrained compared to natural images. The augmentation space for molecular graphs is limited by chemical validity: randomly dropping atoms or edges can produce chemically implausible substructures that provide misleading training signals. Second, the downstream BBBP dataset may be too small and too similar to the pre-training distribution for augmentation-invariant representations to provide substantial benefits.

In contrast, the dual-view approach does not rely solely on graph augmentations for contrastive diversity. The two views are inherently different representations, providing a rich contrastive signal even without augmentations. Augmentations in DVCL serve to further diversify the graph view, but the primary learning signal comes from the cross-view alignment.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

Our findings have several practical implications for molecular property prediction in drug discovery:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Complementary representations are valuable}: The consistent improvement from concatenating graph and fingerprint embeddings (DVCL no-PT vs. DMPNN: +1.4 on BBBP, +3.0 on BACE) demonstrates that these two representation types encode complementary information. Practitioners should consider combining GNN and fingerprint features as a simple and effective strategy.

\item \textbf{Chemistry-aware augmentations matter}: Our ablation shows that chemically motivated augmentations outperform no augmentation, and that combining multiple augmentation types yields the best results. Random augmentations should be avoided in favor of molecular-semantic perturbations.

\item \textbf{Pre-training provides warm-started representations}: Even when pre-training does not dramatically improve final accuracy (as on BBBP), it provides better initialization that can reduce fine-tuning time and improve convergence stability.

\item \textbf{Dual-view pre-training is a general framework}: While we instantiate DVCL with DMPNN and Morgan fingerprints, the dual-view principle is general. Other graph encoders (\eg, GIN, GAT) and other fingerprint types (\eg, MACCS keys, topological torsion fingerprints) could be substituted, providing a flexible framework for molecular self-supervised learning.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of this work should be acknowledged.

\textbf{First}, the HIV and Tox21 experiments are still in progress. The conclusions drawn from BBBP and BACE may not generalize to larger or more challenging benchmarks, particularly the highly imbalanced HIV dataset and the multi-task Tox21 dataset.

\textbf{Second}, our evaluation is limited to four MoleculeNet benchmarks. Additional evaluation on regression tasks (\eg, solubility, lipophilicity) and quantum mechanical properties would strengthen the generalizability of our conclusions.

\textbf{Third}, the DVCL architecture operates on 2D molecular graphs and does not incorporate 3D geometric information such as atom coordinates, bond angles, or torsion angles. Incorporating 3D conformers as an additional contrastive view is a natural extension.

\textbf{Fourth}, the pre-training and fine-tuning stages use separate optimization procedures. End-to-end training with a combined contrastive and supervised objective could potentially yield better representations but would require careful balancing of the two loss terms.

\textbf{Fifth}, the number of pre-training epochs (30) was selected based on BBBP performance and may not be optimal for other downstream tasks. Adaptive pre-training schedules that correlate with downstream validation performance could improve the framework's robustness.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented DVCL, a dual-view contrastive learning framework for molecular property prediction. The key contribution is the use of two inherently different molecular representations---graph structure via DMPNN and chemical fingerprints via MLP---as the basis for self-supervised contrastive pre-training. By aligning these complementary views through an InfoNCE objective, DVCL produces richer molecular representations than either view alone or same-representation contrastive alternatives.

The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item DVCL with pre-training achieves AUROC scores of 0.9245 on BBBP and 0.8875 on BACE, outperforming standard DMPNN without pre-training (0.9102 and 0.8636, respectively) and all standard GNN baselines.

\item The dual-view design outperforms single-view contrastive learning by 0.7 AUROC points on BBBP, validating the advantage of contrasting inherently different representations.

\item Chemistry-aware augmentations---atom masking, edge dropping, and subgraph extraction---contribute to pre-training quality, with subgraph extraction providing the largest individual effect.

\item Concatenating graph and fingerprint embeddings during fine-tuning leverages complementary information, with the DVCL architecture alone (without pre-training) already outperforming standalone DMPNN by 1.4--3.0 AUROC points.
\end{enumerate}

Future work will focus on: (1) completing experiments on HIV and Tox21 to validate generalizability across dataset sizes and task types; (2) incorporating 3D geometric information as a third contrastive view; (3) scaling pre-training to larger unlabeled molecular databases such as ZINC and PubChem; and (4) exploring the dual-view framework for molecular generation and optimization tasks.
